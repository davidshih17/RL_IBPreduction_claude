\title{\bf Symmetry-Enhanced IBP Reduction at Inference:\\
a free routing layer beneath the SAILIR workers}
\author{SAILIR project note}
\date{2026-07-05}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We add graph symmetries as an \emph{inference-time routing layer} beneath the
ML-guided IBP workers of SAILIR. For each non-master integral the layer attempts a
free, search-avoiding symmetry rewrite; only the survivors are handed to a beam-search
worker. On pentagon-box targets this reduces the number of dispatched worker
reductions by $25$--$80\%$ with \emph{no change} to the final master combination.
The construction is only correct and tractable after resolving three obstacles, the
decisive one being that the symmetry rewrites must descend in the \emph{exact same
total order} the workers use---which is what turns a deadlocking, non-confluent
system into a directed acyclic one. The CPU saving is smaller and target-dependent:
symmetry reliably removes the medium-cost bulk of the work, but because it follows a
different reduction path its net benefit depends on whether that path thins or
thickens the expensive hard tail.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup}
SAILIR reduces Feynman integrals to master integrals by dispatching, for each
non-master integral in the running expression, a model-guided beam-search
\emph{worker} that performs one integration-by-parts (IBP) reduction step. The
orchestrator substitutes each worker's result back into the expression and iterates
until only masters remain. The dominant cost is the number of worker calls, each of
which is an expensive search over the IBP relation system.

We work in the two-loop, five-point, all-massless \emph{pentagon-box} family
(``TA''): eight propagators $D_1,\dots,D_8$ and three irreducible scalar products
(ISPs) $D_9,D_{10},D_{11}$. An integral is labelled by its exponent vector
$a\in\mathbb{Z}^{11}$, written $\I{a}$; positive entries are denominator powers,
negative entries are numerator powers.

The workers descend in the total order (matching the training-data target
selection)
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kappa}
\kk(a) \;=\; \bigl(-r,\,-s,\,|a|\bigr),\qquad
r=\!\!\sum_{a_i>0}\! a_i,\quad
s=\!\!\sum_{a_i<0}\!|a_i|,\quad
|a|=(|a_1|,\dots,|a_{11}|),
\end{equation}
with $|a|$ compared lexicographically. \emph{Smaller $\kk$ means higher in the
ordering}: it is the integral the reduction eliminates first. A valid one-step
reduction sends an integral to a combination of strictly-$\kk$-\emph{larger} (i.e.\
lower) integrals; masters may appear at any weight. This ordering---in particular the
sign convention on the $|a|$ tiebreak---is the load-bearing detail of
\S\ref{sec:confluence}.

\section{Symmetries as rewrites}
A symmetry is an affine loop relabeling $g:(k_1,k_2)\mapsto (M(k_1,k_2)+\text{shift})$
that maps the integral family to itself. It induces a linear map on the inverse
propagators,
\begin{equation}
D_i \;\longmapsto\; \sum_j M_{ij}\,D_j + c_i,
\end{equation}
with $c_i$ a kinematic constant, and hence a value identity $\I{a}=\I{g\cdot a}$. For a
\emph{denominator} index the map is (in the useful cases) a single-propagator
relabeling. For a \emph{numerator} index the ISP re-expresses as a linear combination,
and because the numerator is a \emph{product} of such factors, the image of a
degree-$s$ numerator integral is a multinomial: generically a large combination of
same-and-lower-weight integrals. (Concretely, one symmetry image of a degree-3
numerator integral we examined has $103$ terms.)

\paragraph{Rewrite rule.} For a target $\I{a}$ we (i) form the orbit closure of
$\{a\}$ under all applicable symmetries; (ii) collect the exact relations
$\I{K}-\I{g\cdot K}=0$; and (iii) row-reduce them over $\mathrm{GF}(p)$
($p=1009$), pivoting on the $\kk$-\emph{highest} (i.e.\ $\kk$-minimal) integral of each
relation. If $a$ is a pivot, the resulting rule expresses $\I{a}$ as a
$\mathrm{GF}(p)$ combination of strictly-lower (strictly-$\kk$-larger) integrals.

\paragraph{Routing.} At each orchestrator iteration, before dispatching workers, every
non-master $I$ is offered to \code{symmetry\_rule($I$)}. If it returns a rule, the rule
is written into the orchestrator's substitution cache for free (no worker) and the
symmetry descent is cascaded; if it returns \code{None} (a \emph{survivor}) the
integral is dispatched to a worker as usual. Because $P_S$ (the symmetric projector
implicit in these rules) is linear, the substitution machinery is unchanged: symmetry
rules are just additional cache entries.

\section{Three obstacles}

\subsection{Placeholder symmetries}
Kira records some symmetries with an explicit loop substitution (from which $M,c$
follow by momentum algebra) and others as a bare propagator permutation
(``placeholder'') with the loop map omitted. The naive fallback---treat the
placeholder as a clean permutation from its stored image list---is \emph{wrong}: a
propagator listed as ``fixed'' may in truth map to a \emph{combination}. We instead
reconstruct the true relabeling from the placeholder's reliable \emph{swaps} (a
propagator mapping to a \emph{different} propagator), solve for the loop map, and
derive the full $M,c$. This recovers $26$ of $33$ placeholders (the $7$ with no swaps
are dropped), e.g.\ correctly reconstructing $k_1\to-(k_1{+}p_1{+}p_2)$ whose full
map sends $D_2\to D_1-D_2+D_3$. It also eliminates a class of false relations that the
bare-permutation fallback produced (e.g.\ the spurious $\I{2,1,1}=\I{1,1,2}$).

\subsection{Scaleless integrals}
A symmetry can map a nonzero integral onto a scaleless (trivial-sector) one---e.g.\
$D_1D_5D_6\to D_5D_6D_8$, where $k_1$ appears only in $(k_1{-}k_2)^2$ and the
$k_1$-integral is scaleless---which is zero. We do \emph{not} special-case these. Under
the \emph{paper-masters} convention (the production setting) a scaleless corner is not
a master, so a scaleless survivor is dispatched to a worker that reduces it to $0$ by
IBP, exactly as the baseline does (verified directly: the worker returns the empty
expression on such integrals). No explicit zero-sector list is required. (Under the
alternative ``corners-are-masters'' convention, scaleless corners are instead retained
as pseudo-masters, and the symmetry merge breaks the mod-$p$ cancellation the baseline
relies on to make them vanish; hence one must run with paper-masters.)

\subsection{Confluence: the ordering (crux)}
\label{sec:confluence}
The workers descend in $\kk$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa}. A symmetry rewrite \emph{must}
descend in the same order, or the mixed substitution cache is non-confluent.

Our first router pivoted on $(r,s,|a|)$ with the \emph{opposite} $|a|$ tiebreak to
$\kk$. Consider two same-$(r,s)$ (``lateral'') integrals $I,J$ with $I$ below $J$ in
$\kk$. A worker correctly reduces $I\to J$ (down in $\kk$), while the mis-ordered
symmetry reduces $J\to I$. Together they form $I\to J\to I$: the substitution loop
never resolves. In the failing run this hit the $10^4$-iteration substitution cap $29$
times, stranded hundreds of integrals inside the cache (they are cache keys, so the
dispatcher never re-emits them), and the reduction \emph{deadlocked} with $720$
non-masters instead of completing.

\paragraph{Fix.} Use $\kk$ verbatim. The router pivots on the $\kk$-minimal (highest)
integral of each relation, so \emph{every rule edge strictly increases $\kk$}. The
workers' \code{SUCCESS\_TOTAL} test guarantees \emph{every non-master worker edge
strictly increases $\kk$}. Therefore every edge of the combined cache strictly
increases $\kk$: the substitution graph is a directed acyclic graph, and substitution
terminates. This is the whole fix---a one-key change---and it converts the deadlock
into the win below.

\paragraph{Verification.} Over $483$ integrals drawn from a real reduction: $0$ symmetry
rules pointed the wrong way in $\kk$, and $0$ (worker edge $I\to J$, symmetry edge
$J\to I$) reversals existed. Cycles are excluded by construction.

\section{Results}
Each target is reduced twice under identical settings (\code{--paper-masters-only},
prime $1009$, v7 workers), once as baseline and once with \code{--use-symmetry}. The
correctness gate requires the final master combination to be identical.

\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrc}
\toprule
target & baseline & design-1 & routed free & workers saved & gate \\
\midrule
$\I{1,\text{-}1,1,0,1,1}$ (deg 1)                 & $5$    & $1$   & $1$    & $\mathbf{80\%}$ & PASS \\
$\I{2,0,1,0,1,\text{-}1,0,1,\text{-}1,\text{-}1,0}$ (deg 3) & $518$  & $155$ & $186$  & $\mathbf{70\%}$ & PASS \\

\end{tabular}

The ``workers'' columns are dispatched beam-search reductions (the search budget). The
degree-3 target $\I{2,0,1,0,1,-1,0,1,-1,-1,0}$ is the one that deadlocked before the
confluence fix; it now completes, its masters match the baseline, and it uses
$70\%$ fewer workers. Savings are largest on the numerator-heavy targets, where the
symmetry orbits are richest. Worker \emph{count}, however, is an upper bound on the
benefit: \S\ref{sec:cost} looks at the CPU cost, which is smaller than the count
suggests and, on one target, negative.

\section{Why bushier reductions still win}
A symmetry reduction is \emph{not} more compact than an IBP step: for the target
$\I{0,-1,2,0,2,-1,0,1,-1,0,0}$ the symmetry rule has $57$ terms where one IBP step has
$9$, and symmetry cannot reduce every integral at all. Compactness is the wrong metric.
The symmetry step is \emph{free}---no beam search---and its output terms are strictly
lower in $\kk$, so the workers dispose of them cheaply. The quantity that matters is
the total number of dispatched workers (total searches), and that is what drops by
$25$--$80\%$. The free, search-avoiding step paying for its own bushiness is the entire
mechanism of the win.

\section{Cost in detail: worker count vs.\ CPU, and the three-band structure}
\label{sec:cost}
The worker-count reduction overstates the benefit, because symmetry alters the
reduction \emph{path} rather than merely pruning the baseline's tree: on the degree-3
target $\I{2,0,1,0,1,-1,0,1,-1,-1,0}$, $114$ of design-1's $154$ workers ($74\%$) are on
integrals the baseline never reduced. In CPU the outcome is smaller and no longer
uniform:
\begin{itemize}
\item deg-3 $\I{2,0,\dots}$: $7174\to3815$ s ($-47\%$)---a clear win, but smaller than the
      $-70\%$ in worker count;
\item 6-prop $\I{1,1,-1,\dots}$ (partial): $93981\to72593$ s so far---win;
\item deg-3 $\I{1,1,0,1,1,0,-3,\dots}$: $18253$ s (baseline, finished) vs.\ $19949$ s and
      climbing (design-1, still on one hard survivor)---a \emph{loss}.
\end{itemize}
Per worker the \emph{median} is unchanged (identical step counts, comparable time per
step), so the higher \emph{mean} is not overhead but a composition effect: with the
cheap bulk routed away, a few hard survivors dominate (design-1's top-$10$ workers are
$83\%$ of its CPU vs.\ $48\%$ for the baseline).

\paragraph{Three bands.} Binning workers by wall time separates the two effects that
decide the sign of the CPU change. Roughly half of all workers are trivial ($<3$\,s,
$\sim1\%$ of CPU); a \emph{medium} band ($3$--$100$\,s) holds the routable bulk; and a
\emph{hard tail} ($>100$\,s) holds most of the CPU in a handful of workers. The table
gives worker counts (baseline\,$\to$\,design-1) and, for the tail, CPU and the hardest
single worker:
\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
band & $\I{2,0,1,0,1,\text{-}1,\dots}$ & $\I{1,1,\text{-}1,1,1,1,\dots}$ & $\I{1,1,0,1,1,0,\text{-}3,\dots}$ \\
     & (deg 3, \emph{win}) & (6-prop, \emph{win}) & (deg 3, \emph{loss}) \\
\midrule

\bottomrule

Two effects, cleanly separated:
\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{medium band collapses} reliably---symmetry routes those integrals
      away for free ($240\to52$, $1366\to549$). This is the robust, broad-based saving,
      spread over hundreds of workers; excluding the top-$10$ outliers, the deg-3 win's
      bulk CPU falls to $18\%$ of baseline.
\item The \textbf{hard tail is the swing factor.} Because the path differs, the
      $>100$\,s band can shrink in both count and cost ($17\to11$, $186\to113$) or
      \emph{grow} ($30\to33$, with its worst integral jumping $1979\to4311$\,s---an
      integral absent from the baseline entirely).
\end{itemize}
Hence the rule the data gives: \emph{symmetry wins when its reroute thins the hard tail
and loses when it thickens it.} The medium-band collapse is dependable and is the true
source of the broad savings; the hard-tail direction is target-dependent and sets the
sign of the net CPU change.

\section{Implementation}
\begin{itemize}
\item \code{reduction/symmetry\_route.py} --- \code{tkey} ($=\kk$),
      \code{\_build\_rules} (orbit closure + $\kk$-pivoted RREF), \code{symmetry\_rule}.
\item \code{reduction/canonicalize.py} --- applicable transforms; placeholder
      reconstruction via \code{recon\_from\_swaps.py}.
\item \code{reduction/hierarchical\_reduction.py} --- the \code{--use-symmetry}
      cascade (routes free rules, dispatches only survivors).
\item A/B harness: \code{reduction/ab\_run.sh}, \code{ab\_compare.py},
      \code{ab\_status.py}; outputs under \code{results/ab\_symmetry/}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Bottom line.} Inference-time symmetry routing is a net win for SAILIR once
(i) placeholder symmetries are reconstructed, (ii) it is run under paper-masters so the
workers dispose of scaleless integrals, and (iii)---decisively---the rewrites descend
in the workers' own total order $\kk$, which makes the combined cache confluent. The
measured effect is a $25$--$80\%$ reduction in dispatched worker searches with the
master combination unchanged.
